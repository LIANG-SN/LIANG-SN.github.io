The Modern LLM Stack: Lifecycle, Specializations & Roles

How foundation models move from data to production, where multimodal and systems specializations fit, and what the major roles actually own.

Shengnan Liang · Technical overview · Sources checked Oct 26, 2026

01 / The map

Start with the work, then map the roles

An LLM organization rarely looks like a clean assembly line. Data, pretraining, post-training, evaluation, inference, product work, and operations overlap, and the same decision can surface in several places. A longer context window, for example, changes training data, evaluation design, KV-cache pressure, serving cost, and the product experiences that are practical.

The lifecycle is still the clearest place to start because every stage produces something the next stage can use: a dataset, a checkpoint, a scorecard, an endpoint, or production evidence. It also exposes a common mistake in discussions of “the model.” An excellent checkpoint can still produce a poor product when retrieval misses the right document, a tool call fails, tail latency is too high, or the system has no safe way to recover.

Lifecycle alone hides a second kind of structure. Teams may specialize by what the work is about—speech, video, agents, safety, long context, edge deployment—or by the artifact they own. That is why this map does not force every stage into symmetric research and engineering columns. Some intersections support a dedicated research group; others are mainly engineering work or shared responsibility.

02 / Build the model

Data creates the signal; pretraining creates the base model

Model behavior starts in the data, long before anyone writes a post-training objective. Data teams turn permitted raw sources into versioned corpora through filtering, deduplication, tokenization, mixture design, annotation, and quality checks. The mix matters as much as the raw volume: more code, multilingual text, dialogue, images, or synthetic reasoning traces changes what the model learns and where it remains weak. Corpus and data engineers build the pipelines; data researchers study quality and mixture choices; human-data teams define and operate annotation programs.

Good data work also creates boundaries. Provenance and licensing need to be traceable. Contamination checks protect benchmarks from leaking into training. Held-out evaluation sets stay separate, and every dataset version should be reproducible enough to explain a regression months later. For multimodal models, the same problems extend to image–text pairing, speech segmentation, video sampling, sensor alignment, and modality balance.

Pretraining turns that corpus into a base checkpoint. Model researchers choose architectures and objectives and study scaling behavior; research engineers turn promising ideas into experiments that can survive a full run. Training-systems, distributed-systems, and kernel engineers keep thousands of accelerators useful through parallelism, communication, checkpointing, fault recovery, memory management, and performance profiling. At frontier scale, a modeling idea that cannot run reliably is not yet a result.

The boundary between research and infrastructure is therefore practical rather than ceremonial. A change to attention, mixture-of-experts routing, optimizer state, or sequence length may begin as a model hypothesis and end as a new kernel, parallelization strategy, and monitoring requirement. The Llama 3 report gives a concrete model-development example. Anthropic's public JDs for pretraining data infrastructure and pretraining research engineering, along with Megatron Core, show the handoff between data, experiments, and distributed execution.

03 / Post-training

SFT, preference learning, and RL use different signals

A base model is trained to predict its data well. A product needs more specific behavior: follow an instruction, respect a format, decline unsafe requests, use a tool, recover from an error, or spend more computation on a hard problem. Post-training supplies those behavioral signals. A production recipe often combines the methods below and may revisit them as new failures appear; there is no universal sequence.

Common post-training methods
MethodLearning signalWhat the team actually builds
SFT
Supervised fine-tuning		Demonstrations of desired responses or trajectories; typically a token-level likelihood objective.Instruction datasets, conversation formatting, loss masks, tool-use examples, and regression evaluations.
DPO
Direct preference optimization		Preferred versus dispreferred responses, optimized directly with a preference objective.Preference pairs, reference-policy handling, data audits, and preference/generalization checks. Standard offline DPO does not require an online rollout-and-reward-model loop.
RLHF / RLAIFRewards informed by human or AI feedback, often through a learned preference model.Reward models or graders, sampled responses, policy updates, and checks for reward exploitation. The feedback source does not by itself specify the optimizer.
RL with verifiable rewardsProgrammatic checks such as answer correctness or executable tests.Task generators, verifiers, sandboxed environments, rollout pipelines, and policy optimization. A passing test remains a proxy for the intended task.

The choice depends on the signal available. SFT is natural when experts can demonstrate the behavior directly. Preference methods help when ranking two answers is easier than writing the ideal one. RL becomes attractive when the model can explore a sequence of actions and receive a useful reward, especially for code, mathematics, tool use, and other tasks with checkable outcomes. In practice, the hard part is often the data or environment around the optimizer: ambiguous labels, narrow graders, exploitable rewards, and unrealistic sandboxes all teach the wrong lesson.

DPO, RLHF, and DeepSeek-R1 give concrete examples, but their names do not define an org chart. Post-training and RL researchers design data, rewards, and objectives; research engineers implement and debug the recipes; RL-systems teams operate sampling, environments, verifiers, weight updates, and evaluation. Because RL repeatedly generates trajectories, it is also a demanding inference workload. Anthropic's RL Engineering and Production Model Post-Training JDs make that overlap visible.

The deliverable is more than a checkpoint with a higher training reward. Teams need independent evidence of task success, robustness, and regression risk, with hidden tests and graders kept outside the policy's control. A model that has learned to please one reward model may still be less useful to people.

04 / Evaluate and ship

A checkpoint becomes useful only through a reliable system

Evaluation is the measurement system for the entire stack. It runs before, during, and after training and separates questions that are easy to blur together: Did the model know the answer? Did retrieval supply the right evidence? Did the tool execute correctly? Was the response safe, fast, and affordable? Evaluation researchers work on valid measurements and hard test sets; evaluation engineers build repeatable harnesses and regression dashboards; safety researchers, red teams, security engineers, and domain experts probe different failure modes. When model-based judges are used, they still need calibration against human decisions or objective checks. Anthropic's Post-Training Model Evaluations JD is one public example of this work.

Inference and serving turn a checkpoint into an endpoint. The engineering spans kernels, quantization, batching, KV-cache management, scheduling, routing, and capacity planning. Its objective is workload-specific: an offline batch job values throughput, a voice assistant cares about time to first token and interruption latency, and an agent may need cheap repeated calls with a large cached context. Quantization, pruning, and distillation trade model size or precision against quality, memory, latency, power, and cost. A single tokens-per-second number hides too much; useful results report a quality–latency–cost curve for a stated workload. vLLM, SGLang, and NVIDIA's Inference Performance Engineer JD illustrate this layer.

The product layer adds retrieval, tools, agents, permissions, state, and a user experience around the endpoint. Applied ML and product engineers own whether the complete task succeeds, including the parts the model never sees. Platform engineers and SREs make releases repeatable, control capacity, and provide rollback and incident response. Edge teams solve the same problem under tighter memory, power, thermal, connectivity, and latency limits; they may partition work between a device and the cloud rather than serve the original model unchanged.

Monitoring closes the loop. Ordinary service metrics—availability, queue depth, errors, and tail latency—show whether the system is running. Behavioral signals—task completion, groundedness, tool failures, safety events, user corrections, and drift—show whether it is still useful. Those signals need privacy-aware collection and careful interpretation before they become new data, evaluations, serving changes, or product fixes. Ray Serve's production and monitoring guides describe the operational side.

These layers influence one another. The DeepSeek-V4 report, for example, connects attention design with long-context KV-cache size and inference cost. Speech and video models add streaming and high-bandwidth inputs; agent systems turn inference into a stateful loop; on-device models make compression and hardware–software co-design central. For a deeper provider comparison at the serving layer, see Mapping the AI Inference Stack.

Titles vary widely across companies. “Research Engineer” may mean rapid implementation of new model ideas at one lab and distributed training infrastructure at another. “Applied Scientist” may own post-training experiments, a retrieval system, or an end-to-end product metric. Even “ML Engineer” can sit anywhere from data pipelines to serving.

A better way to read a job description is to look for the owned artifact, the success metric, the failure mode that becomes harder at scale, and the neighboring teams. Owning a training recipe points toward experimentation and model quality; owning cluster utilization points toward training systems; owning p99 latency and cost points toward inference; owning task completion and user feedback points toward applied ML. The research–engineering label is useful only after those details are clear.

如何理解这张 LLM 技术栈地图

第一维是生命周期：数据 → 预训练 → 后训练 → 评测与发布 → 推理与服务 → 产品与部署 → 监控与反馈。它不是单向流水线：评测贯穿全程，RL 本身需要大规模推理 rollout，线上问题也会反向推动数据、模型和系统迭代。一个 checkpoint 很强，并不代表最终产品一定好；检索、工具调用、延迟、权限和故障恢复都可能决定真实体验。

第二维是工作对象：文本、视觉、语音、视频与具身交互；推理、代码、RAG、Agent、长上下文；安全、可解释性与隐私；以及云端、边缘、硬件、压缩和实时约束。第三维才是岗位 ownership。看职位时，应结合阶段 × 专业方向 × 交付物：具体负责什么产物、用什么指标衡量、规模扩大后最难的问题是什么。Research / Engineering 的 title 只能提供有限信息，也没有必要为每个阶段强行对应一个 research 岗位。
